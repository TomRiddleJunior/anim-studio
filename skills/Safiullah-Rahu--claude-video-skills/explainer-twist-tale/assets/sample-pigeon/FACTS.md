# "The Missile Steered by a Pigeon" — fact sheet (checked 29 Oct 2026)

Every fact line in the narration maps to a row. Framing lines ("Sharp eyes, quick turns", "And that's what sank it",
"So the pigeons were never the problem") carry no new facts; they summarise the rows below.

| # | Fact as used | Source |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | In spring 1940 B. F. Skinner, a psychologist at the University of Minnesota, was on a train to Chicago when he watched a flock of birds flying alongside the train; he saw them as "devices" with excellent vision and manoeuvrability and asked whether they could guide a missile | Skinner's autobiography (1979), as quoted in Benjamin Schultz-Figueroa, *The Celluloid Specimen* (University of California Press), excerpt: https://www.engadget.com/hitting-the-books-the-celluloid-specimen-benjamin-schultz-figueroa-university-of-california-press-143028555.html |
| P2 | Pigeons were trained by being shown an image of the target and rewarded with grain for pecking it, with faster pecking required over time | Wikipedia, "Project Pigeon" https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Pigeon ; Steve Mirsky, "Pigeons as Pilots?", *Scientific American*, 1 Sep 2008 https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/pigeons-as-pilots/ |
| P3 | "One bird pecked more than 10,000 times in 45 minutes" | Wikipedia, "Project Pigeon"; migflug.com, "The Bomb Steered by Live Pigeons" https://migflug.com/afterburner/project-pigeon-skinner-guided-bomb/ |
| P4 | The guidance was built into the nose of the Pelican, a glide bomb (an unpowered guided missile) | B. F. Skinner, "Pigeons in a Pelican", *American Psychologist* 15 (1960): 28–37; Scientific American (a "gliding missile"); Wikipedia (unpowered glide-bomb airframe) |
| P5 | Lenses in the nose projected the view ahead onto a screen; the pigeon pecked at the target's image; the screen was on pivots with sensors (pneumatic valves in Skinner's design), so when the image moved off centre the pecks sent correcting signals to the control surfaces | Wikipedia, "Project Pigeon"; Schultz-Figueroa (Skinner: an image "as little as a quarter of an inch off-center" produced a correction); migflug.com |
| P6 | One to three pigeons per nose; with three, the system followed the majority, and a bird that disagreed was "punished for his minority opinion" | Wikipedia, "Project Pigeon"; migflug.com; Skinner (1960) |
| P7 | The National Defense Research Committee saw the idea as "very eccentric and impractical" but still contributed $25,000 (General Mills had earlier given $5,000) | Wikipedia, "Project Pigeon"; Scientific American ($25,000 "from the feds"; $5,000 from General Mills) |
| P8 | At a final demonstration a live pigeon performed in front of a government committee; Skinner wrote that the spectacle only reminded the committee how fantastic the proposal was | Scientific American (Mirsky, quoting Skinner); Schultz-Figueroa (quoting Skinner) |
| P9 | The project was dropped during the war | Wikipedia (cancelled 8 Oct 1944); Schultz-Figueroa (the Army denied further funds in Dec 1943) — the sources disagree on the date, so the film gives none |
| P10 | The Navy revived the work in 1948 as Project ORCON ("organic control") and tested it until 1953 | Wikipedia, "Project Pigeon"; Schultz-Figueroa (US Naval Research Laboratory); migflug.com — the sources disagree on why it ended, so the film gives no reason |
| P11 | In September 2024 Skinner was posthumously awarded the Ig Nobel Peace Prize for experiments on housing live pigeons inside missiles to guide them; his daughter, Julie Vargas, accepted it | Associated Press, 12 Sep 2024 (e.g. https://www.clickondetroit.com/news/weird-news/2024/09/12/studies-on-pigeon-guided-missiles-swimming-abilities-of-dead-fish-among-ig-nobles-winners/); Smithsonian Magazine Smart News 180985090 |
| P12 | Skinner complained that "our problem was no one would take us seriously" | Wikipedia, "Project Pigeon" |

On screen: the train, the lab, the hangar, the cut-away nose, the committee room, the dock and the prize stage are
invented, generic sets; the target ship on the screens is a generic drawing (no real vessel); the peck counter and the
"2–1" tally are illustrations of P3 and P6. The stamps ECCENTRIC and IMPRACTICAL show P7's wording; the $25,000 cheque
is a drawing. Nobody real is drawn: Skinner, the committee, the sailors and the officials are generic toon figures;
the prize stage shows a trophy only (no ceremony, no logo, no people).

Deliberately left out: the cancellation year (P9 conflict), the reason ORCON ended (P10 conflict), Skinner's exact
quotes on screen, the General Mills amount, any claim that a pigeon-guided bomb was ever dropped, pigeon casualty
figures, and the Smithsonian nose cone's display status (its catalogue says not on view).
