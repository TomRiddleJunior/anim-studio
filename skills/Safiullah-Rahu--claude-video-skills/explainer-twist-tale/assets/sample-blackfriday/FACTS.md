# "Why Black Friday Is Called Black Friday" — fact sheet (checked 29 Oct 2026)

Every fact line in the narration maps to a row. Framing lines ("cops who dreaded it", "a better story", "the version
stores loved", "the rebrand that finally worked") carry no new facts; they summarise the rows below.

| # | Fact as used | Source |
|---|---|---|
| B1 | The name came from Philadelphia's police: the traffic squad called the day after Thanksgiving "Black Friday" (and the next day "Black Saturday") for its Center City traffic jams, in the early 1960s | Philadelphia Inquirer, "Black Friday origins" (archive piece quoting Joseph P. Barrett, 1994: "The term 'Black Friday' came out of the old Philadelphia Police Department's traffic squad"); Wikipedia "Black Friday (shopping)" |
| B2 | "Every Black Friday, no traffic policeman was permitted to take the day off", and even the police band was ordered to Center City | Philadelphia Inquirer (Barrett account) |
| B3 | Downtown filled with shoppers and with out-of-town visitors for the Army–Navy football game, played in Philadelphia on the Saturday | Philadelphia Inquirer; wordorigins.org "Black Friday / Cyber Monday" |
| B4 | In 1961 the city and its merchants wanted a nicer name; a public-relations expert suggested "Big Friday" and "Big Saturday"; it was quickly forgotten and reporters kept using "Black Friday" | Wikipedia "Black Friday (shopping)" (citing Public Relations News, 18 Dec 1961); Philadelphia Inquirer (Barrett) |
| B5 | The earliest known published "in the black" explanation (the day stores turn from losses to profit) appeared in the Philadelphia Inquirer in November 1981 — some twenty years after the police usage | Wikipedia "Black Friday (shopping)"; wordorigins.org |
| B6 | The term spread nationally only slowly: a 1985 Inquirer report found retailers in Cincinnati and Los Angeles still unaware of it | Wikipedia "Black Friday (shopping)" |
| B7 | Black Friday 2026 falls on Friday 27 November (Thanksgiving is Thursday 26 November 2026) | calendar: US Thanksgiving = fourth Thursday of November |

On screen: the department store, "The Morning Paper", its 1981 headline and every ledger figure are invented and
generic (no real masthead, store or account); the tower at the end of Broad Street is a simplified, generic stand-in;
Broad St and Market St are real Center City streets; the "1960s" photo is a drawing. The tear-off calendar shows
weekday names only, no dates. Nobody real is drawn: the cops, reporters, PR man and shop owners are generic toon figures.

Deliberately left out: the 1869 gold-market crash and the "slave sale" myth (unrelated or false), the 1951 factory-absence
usage (didn't catch on), "12-hour shifts" (sources differ on what was required), any number of shoppers or cars.
