# FACTS — "Survival Odds · The Rig · Day 1"

The film is fiction: the dead do not exist, and the platform, Port Ness, the shore base and Mira are invented. No real
operator, field, company, flag or logo appears; every sign carries generic words only. This file notes which details are
real-world plausible and which are the story's own. Checked 9 Nov 2026.

## The planted answer (never said in the film)
On **this platform**, the platform is operated from the shore, and it shuts itself down when the shore link has been lost
for 72 hours: no gas, so no power, so no watermaker, no heat, no lights.

| claim | status | notes / sources |
|---|---|---|
| Small offshore gas platforms can be *normally unmanned* and run from a control room elsewhere (onshore or on another platform), with crews flown in for maintenance | **True in general** | Remote operation of unmanned or periodically manned platforms is established practice in the North Sea. Premier Oil's Solan was planned to be controlled from an onshore control room via a satellite link with monthly visits; Equinor's (then Statoil's) Valemon was built to be periodically unmanned and run from shore. [NOIA: Offshore oil industry experiments with remote control](https://www.noia.org/offshore-oil-industry-experiments-with-remote-control/) · [Offshore magazine: Integrated system enables oil platform to be remotely controlled](https://www.offshore-mag.com/production/article/16757007/integrated-system-enables-oil-platform-to-be-remotely-controlled) |
| A remotely operated platform can be designed to shut down if it loses contact with its control room | **True for some installations** | Valemon's design shuts the installation down automatically if it loses contact with the main control room; two independent communication routes guard against that. Safety systems in this industry are generally *fail-safe* (a lost signal or lost power trips the process to a safe state). [SINTEF / HFC: Remote operation of Valemon](https://www.sintef.no/globalassets/project/hfc/documents/5-gjertsen-remote-operation-of-valemon-2pp.pdf) |
| "72 hours" before the shutdown | **Story figure** | The delay is the story's choice for this fictional platform, written on its door sticker (plant 3). Real delays, where they exist, are set per installation. The film never claims every rig works this way. |
| The shore control room closing at 23:59 (plant 2) breaks the link | **Story logic** | Fiction: if nobody keeps the shore end running, the link is lost. |

## The other claims on screen
| line | status | notes |
|---|---|---|
| "a small gas platform … a hundred and forty kilometres out at sea" | Plausible | Offshore gas fields lie from tens to a few hundred kilometres from the coast. In the picture the coast is drawn much closer (a stylised horizon); the HUD map carries the 140 km. |
| "Most nights, nobody lives here at all." | Plausible | That is what *normally unmanned* means (see above). |
| "The dead can't swim. They sink." | **Story rule**, with a real basis | The dead are fictional. Drowned bodies usually sink at first because the lungs fill with water; decomposition gases can float a body back up later, which takes days in warm water and weeks or longer in cold, deep water (some never resurface). This is also a fair *rival answer* for the comments ("they float back up"). [IFLScience: Do dead bodies sink or float?](https://www.iflscience.com/do-dead-bodies-sink-or-float-75419) · [Science ABC: Why do corpses float?](https://www.scienceabc.com/eyeopeners/why-do-corpses-float.html) |
| "Emergency food for twelve people for a month. For one person, that's almost a year." | Arithmetic | 12 × 30 = 360 person-days ≈ one person for a year. Survival stores for a visiting crew stranded by weather are plausible; the quantity is the story's. |
| "A machine that turns seawater into drinking water." | True | Reverse-osmosis watermakers are standard on ships and offshore installations. |
| "The platform burns its own gas. Forever." | Plausible, then the twist | Gas-fired generators running on the platform's own produced gas (fuel gas) are common on larger installations; a small unmanned platform may instead use cables, batteries or solar. "Forever" is the character's belief: the shutdown (the answer) ends it. |
| The flare | Plausible | Many installations burn off gas on a flare boom; on a small unmanned platform it would be small or only used in upsets. Here it is a constant visual (and a rival answer: "the only light for 100 km, boats will come"). |
| "A helicopter is just a very fast way to land in the middle of it." | Story reasoning | Offshore crews do travel by helicopter from coastal heliports; a 140 km leg is well within a typical medium helicopter's range. |

## Rival answers the film leaves open (good for comments)
The flare (it attracts boats or survivors), refusing the helicopter, the storms, the gas itself (explosion), the seagull,
the dead floating back up (see above), the coast going dark (no rescue). The joke blank: "she forgot ___".
